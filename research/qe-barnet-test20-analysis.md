## PART 1 — ENGLISH (60 marks / 45 minutes / 60 questions, all A–E)

### Section structure (differs from our platform's 28/9/9/8 GL structure)

| Section | Qs | Marks | Format |

**Key structural difference from our GL-format mocks**: QE Barnet runs comprehension across *two separate, tonally different passages* (one literary fiction, one expository non-fiction) rather than one passage, and the sentence-completion section is **one continuous narrative extract with 10 gaps threaded through it**, not 8 standalone sentences. If replicating this exactly, that's the two biggest format deltas to build for.

### Passage 1 analysis: "Only Truth" (fiction, first-person)

- **Genre**: literary fiction, first-person present-tense narration, domestic/marital psychological drama.
- **Length**: 5 paragraphs, ~450 words.
- **Narrative voice**: **unreliable narrator** — this is the passage's central device and Q10, Q12 both test it directly. The narrator repeatedly undercuts her own claims ("I know I'm being petty", "If he has a fault, it is that he's always, always right" — the conditional "If" signals she won't commit to naming it a flaw even while describing something flaw-like).
- **Structural arc**: para 1 (physical state — headache, sensory overload) → para 2 (waking beside husband, established routine/history) → para 3 (observing husband from doorway, physical description) → para 4 (character assessment of husband, tinged with self-doubt) → para 5 (backstory — mother-in-law's disapproval, marriage, closing resolve to "face today with optimism").
- **Devices planted for later questions**: onomatopoeia/alliteration/simile/personification all stacked in paragraph 1 specifically so Q1 can ask "which is NOT present" (foreshadowing is correctly absent — nothing in para 1 hints at what's coming, which is itself notable since the passage's title "Only Truth" implies concealment is the theme). Oxymoron ("a bed full of emptiness") is planted in para 2 and tested at Q7. The word "mostly" (para 4) is a deliberately loaded modifier tested at Q9 as an implicit-meaning question.
- **Domain knowledge cross-over**: Q3 requires knowing axons are part of the nervous system — a science-general-knowledge question wrapped inside a comprehension quote, not answerable from the passage text alone.
- **Emotional throughline for inference questions**: the narrator's self-worth relative to her husband (Q5, Q9, Q11, Q12) and her relationship with her own parents, established only via one throwaway sentence in para 5 (Q14) — a single-sentence, easy-to-miss detail question.

#### Passage 1 question-by-question (Q1–15)

|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | Vocabulary-in-context | "dust motes" = specks | Easy | Distractors are near-miss physical descriptions (balls/layers/sprinklings) of dust, not unrelated words |

### Passage 2 analysis: expository non-fiction ("Titanic" essay, mislabelled "Tom Sawyer" in this edition)

- **Genre**: persuasive/analytical non-fiction essay — historical argument, not narrative.
- **Length**: 6 paragraphs (para 1 very short — a single rhetorical question), ~500 words.
- **Structure**: opens with a **rhetorical question** as thesis-setup (para 1–2), builds an evidence chain allocating blame (para 3), pivots to the "size = safety fetish" argument (para 4–5), closes with confidence-in-safety-claims material (para 6).
- **Register**: formal, editorial, uses period-appropriate vocabulary (preeminent, leviathans, stupefaction) deliberately chosen to test vocabulary-in-context (Q16, Q18, Q21, Q23) alongside comprehension.
- **Argumentative technique tested directly**: Q19 asks what the author did *NOT* blame — this only works because the passage explicitly allocates blame to 3 named causes (navigation, lifeboats, nearby captain's inaction) and the "correct" wrong-to-blame option (passengers) is never mentioned at all, meaning the student must hold a mental checklist of everything the author DID say to correctly identify the omission.
- **Author-voice inference questions** (Q20, Q22, Q30) ask "which word would the author be most likely to use" — these require inferring authorial *attitude* (critical of complacency, not neutral reporting) from tone across the whole passage, not a single quote.
- **Source-criticism/history-skill question** (Q28) is a meta-cognitive question about historiography — asks the student to reason about the passage as a *historical source*, a skill borrowed from history curricula, not English.

| Q | Type | Tests | Difficulty | Distractor design |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16 | Vocabulary-in-context | "preeminent" = most important | Easy | Standard synonym set, one plausible near-miss ("insightful") |
| 17 | Grammar — counting a part of speech | 3 adjectives in a quoted sentence | Standard | Requires correctly parsing "latest/largest/safest" as adjectives, not nouns |
| 18 | Vocabulary-in-context (idiom/phrase) | "lay mind" = non-expert | Standard | Distractors conflate "lay" with unrelated senses (idle, uneducated, active) — precision trap |
| 19 | NOT-question requiring negative-space recall | Author does NOT blame passengers | Hard | The 4 wrong options are all *genuinely* blamed in the text (must be individually verified); the right answer is something never mentioned at all — hardest question type in the section |
| 20 | Author-voice inference | Author would call travel "dicey" | Standard | Requires synthesising tone (the author frames travel as risky throughout), not one quote |
| 21 | Vocabulary-in-context | "stupefaction" = astonishment | Standard | Distractor "stupidity" is a false-cognate trap (same root, wrong meaning) |
| 22 | Author-voice inference | Author would call captain "complacent" | Hard | Distractors are all negative-adjacent words (criminal/crafty/cranky/clueless) — student must pick the *precise* shade of criticism the text supports (overconfidence, not malice or incompetence) |

**Pattern to replicate**: non-fiction passage questions lean far more on vocabulary-in-context (6 of 15) and author-voice inference (4 of 15) than the fiction passage did, and include a genuine NOT-question (19) plus a cross-subject question (24, maths-adjacent date arithmetic) and a meta-cognitive source-criticism question (28) — variety of *question genre*, not just difficulty, is what makes this section feel different from Passage 1 despite similar structure.

### Punctuation section (Q31–40) — segment "find the mistake" format

Format: sentence split into groups A/B/C/D, student picks the group containing the error, or N for no mistake. Same rendering convention as our platform's `SegmentMistakeAnswer`.

| Q | Error type | Rule tested |
|---|---|---|
| 31 | Hyphenation of two-word numbers | "thirty three" should be "thirty-three" (21–99 compound numbers need hyphens) |
| 32 | Incorrect quotation marks around non-speech | Quoting a paraphrased instruction ("to sit down") that isn't verbatim speech |
| 33 | **No mistake** — correct comma-after-introductory-phrase + parenthetical hyphen use | Control question testing whether students over-flag correct sentences |
| 34 | Missing comma to offset an interrupting clause | "if you wish to do so" needs bracketing commas |
| 35 | Semicolon misused for a mid-clause interrupter | "would; therefore, like" — semicolon should be a comma since "therefore" doesn't start an independent clause |
| 36 | Hyphenation of a written-out fraction | "One third" → "One-third" |
| 37 | Comma incorrectly inserted after a non-introductory phrase | "It's funny, how..." — no comma needed since what follows isn't a complete independent clause |
| 38 | Apostrophe placement for joint possession | "Lucas' and Akemi's" should drop the apostrophe on the first name in joint ownership |
| 39 | Missing comma before interrupted direct speech | No comma after "explained" before the quote resumes |
| 40 | Semicolon before a coordinating conjunction | "rate; yet they" — "yet" is a coordinating conjunction, only needs a comma |

**Pattern to replicate**: exactly 1 of the 10 is a genuine "no mistake" (10%, consistent with our own skill's 1–2-per-9 guidance). Errors span apostrophes, hyphenation, comma splices, semicolon misuse, and quotation-mark misuse — no single error type repeats, and difficulty is fairly flat (no obvious easy-to-hard ramp within this section, unlike the comprehension sections).

| Q | Misspelling → correct | Word category |
|---|---|---|

| 42 | motif → motive | Homophone-adjacent confusable (real word, wrong word) |

Kaye, used as unattributed continuous text for spelling+cloze). The mix is: 1 irregular plural, 2 vowel/phonetic traps, 1 silent-letter trap, 1 prefix/word-family confusion, 1 adverb-formation error, 1 US/UK spelling convention, 1 uncommon vocabulary word, 1 proper-noun/loanword — again exactly 1 of 10 is "no mistake" (10%).

### Sentence Completion section (Q51–60) — continuous-narrative multi-gap cloze

This is the format most different from our platform's cloze section. Instead of 8 independent one-sentence gaps, QE Barnet threads **10 gaps through one continuous narrative passage** (same "Far Pavilions" extract as the spelling section, revealing the two sections share source text — a real economy-of-content technique worth noting). Each gap has 5 word/phrase options.

| Q | Gap logic tested |
|---|---|
| 51 | Correct part of speech + collocation — "a pass" (singular noun matching mountain-geography context), distractors are other forms of the same root word (passing/past/passed/passages) |
| 52 | Adverb choice matching connotation — "manfully" fits "lusty cries", distractors are adverbs of the wrong emotional register (pathetically/doubtfully/fearfully) |
| 53 | Noun choice matching setting — "mountains" (established as Himalayas), distractors are other landscape nouns that don't fit the stated location |
| 54 | Noun choice matching sensory logic — "reek" (bad smell) needed to contrast with "clean scent", distractors are near-synonyms of smell but wrong connotation, or unrelated nouns |
| 55 | Compound adjective+noun choice matching temperature logic — "icy draught" must match "cold air" earlier in sentence; distractors mismatch either the adjective (temperate/cool — wrong temperature) or the noun spelling (draft vs draught — deliberately tests UK spelling of "draught") |
| 56 | Adjective choice matching a logical deduction the character is making — "premature" is the only option consistent with the character's admitted "miscalculation" of due date |
| 57 | Idiom/collocation completion — "cost her dear" is a fixed idiomatic phrase; distractors are literal paraphrases of the same *meaning* (be expensive / lose her money) that are grammatically fine but not the idiomatic collocation actually required |
| 58 | Preposition + fixed-phrase choice — "called upon" is a fixed phrasal-verb construction; distractors are other prepositions that don't form a real idiom with "called" in this sense |
| 59 | Pronoun agreement with a plural antecedent — "those" (of Victoria and Albert) needs a plural, non-proximal demonstrative; distractors fail either the plural or the "not nearby" requirement |
| 60 | Adverb-modifying-adjective vs coordinate-adjective test — "shockingly unconventional" needs an adverb (not another comma-separated adjective) to modify the following adjective; distractors are adjectives that would require a comma, or the wrong article agreement (an/a) |

**Pattern to replicate**: this section is the hardest to author well because every gap must work in the context of a *single flowing narrative*, not isolated sentences — get the register wrong once (word 51) and every later gap's "obviously correct" answer becomes harder to write cleanly. The logic types used, in order: noun/word-form choice → adverb-connotation → noun/setting-logic → noun/sensory-contrast → compound adjective+spelling → adjective/logical-deduction → idiom-collocation → phrasal-verb/preposition → pronoun-agreement → adverb-vs-adjective. Notice it deliberately never repeats the same *grammatical* logic type twice in the 10 gaps — that variety, not raw difficulty, is what makes the section feel comprehensive rather than repetitive.

## PART 1 SUMMARY — What to replicate structurally for the next English mock in this style

1. **Two passages, not one** — one literary-fiction narrative (character-driven, unreliable-narrator-friendly), one expository/persuasive non-fiction (argument-driven, vocabulary-in-context-heavy). This alone doubles the *range* of comprehension skill tested versus our single-passage GL format.
2. **5 options (A–E), not 4** — every comprehension, punctuation, and spelling question needs a 5th distractor. For punctuation/spelling this is the "N — no mistake" option (~10% of items should genuinely have no error). For comprehension/cloze the 5th option should be a genuine near-miss, not a throwaway.
3. **At least one genuine NOT-question per passage** ("does NOT contain", "did NOT blame") — these are structurally the hardest item type since the student must verify 4 true statements to find the one false/absent one, and they test comprehensiveness of reading, not just recall of one detail.
4. **Author-voice / character-voice inference questions** ("which word would the author/narrator be most likely to use") appear repeatedly (Q5, 12, 20, 22, 30) — this is a distinct, reusable question archetype worth adding explicitly to our own comprehension toolkit alongside retrieval/inference/vocabulary/grammar/literary-technique.
5. **Cross-subject hybrid questions** are used sparingly but deliberately (Q3 = biology, Q24 = date arithmetic) — a light touch of general knowledge or numeracy embedded in an English question, not the whole section.
6. **Grammar questions are evenly spaced as "breather" items** between harder inference/vocabulary questions, not clustered.
7. **Vocabulary-in-context questions on the non-fiction passage lean into genuinely obscure/period words** (preeminent, leviathans, stupefaction) rather than everyday near-synonyms — appropriate difficulty ramp for the "harder passage" of the pair.
8. **The spelling and sentence-completion sections can share one underlying source text** (both drawn from the same real-novel extract here) — an efficient technique: write one strong ~150-word narrative extract, then build the spelling-mistake sentences AND the cloze gaps from within it, rather than authoring two unrelated blocks of content.

This paper is a **Primary-Maths-Challenge-style reasoning paper**, not a standard-arithmetic paper — almost no question is "compute this directly"; nearly every one requires a trick, a structural insight, or a counterexample search. This is meaningfully harder than our own GL-format Maths mocks and closer in spirit to our `maths-elite-6`+ "Summit Stretch" reasoning-heavy papers.

### Maths paper patterns to replicate

1. **Almost every question is reasoning-based, not computation-based** — even "simple" topics (fractions, percentages, ratio) are dressed in a structural trick (reverse percentages, weighted averages, comparing via distance-from-1) rather than tested as direct arithmetic. This is a materially different design philosophy from a standard-arithmetic paper.
2. **"Which statement is true/false" questions appear 3 times** (Q18, 28, 60) as a distinct recurring archetype — each presents 4 abstract number-property claims and requires disproving 3 via targeted counterexamples. Worth adding explicitly as a Maths question archetype.
3. **Geometry questions consistently avoid direct formula plug-in** — they require a structural insight first (shared-base triangles, area-ratio-from-linear-ratio traps, trapezium decomposition) before any arithmetic happens.
4. **Real-world context questions are still reasoning-heavy**, not just "plug numbers into a formula" (pool surface area, bacterial doubling, coin-weight-and-value simultaneous constraints) — every "word problem" hides at least one non-obvious step.
5. **A "not enough information" / "depends on the solution" correct answer appears once** (Q59) as a deliberate trap for students who assume every question has a single deterministic numeric ratio — worth including sparingly in our own papers as a genre of question, not just a rare edge case.
6. **Difficulty is heavily back-loaded** — Q1–15 are mostly Standard, Q16 onward is overwhelmingly Hard, with only occasional "breather" Standard questions (27, 29, 34, 37, 39, 48, 53, 55, 56) scattered through the back half. This is a steeper ramp than our own Elite Maths papers, which spread stretch-difficulty more evenly.
