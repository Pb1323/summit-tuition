Understanding Setting — Part 2

Name: ______________Date: ______________Time: 15 min
Quick recap from Part 1: Read setting in 3 layers — Physical detail → Sensory language → Mood/effect. Keep using this every time. Part 2 adds two more tools for the same job.

1 · Copy This Down — Setting as a Signal

Tool 1 — Pathetic Fallacy

When a writer gives weather or nature human feelings, to match a character's mood. E.g. "the sky pressed down, heavy and resentful" when a character is upset.

Tool 2 — Contrast and Change of Setting

When a passage shows two different settings (or the same setting before/after), the contrast itself is the clue. Ask: what changed, and what does that change tell us about time passing, danger increasing, or a character's feelings shifting?

Tool 3 — Setting as Foreshadowing

A setting detail that seems small can be planted early to hint at what happens later (a locked door, a storm building, an unusually quiet street). Always ask: could this detail be a hint, not just decoration?

Method — use all 3 together

  1. Run the 3 layers from Part 1 first (physical → sensory → mood)
  2. Check: is nature/weather being given human feelings? (pathetic fallacy)
  3. Check: has the setting changed or contrasted with an earlier one? What does the change signal?
  4. Check: could any small detail be foreshadowing something later in the passage?

2 · Worked Example

Earlier in a passage: "The square was bright and busy, stallholders calling out over each other." Later: "By the time she returned, the square had emptied. A single shutter banged, once, somewhere out of sight, and nobody came to close it."

Contrast Busy/bright → empty/silent — the change itself signals something has gone wrong, before the passage says so directly.

Foreshadowing "nobody came to close it" hints that something has interrupted the normal routine of the square — a clue for what's coming.

Pathetic fallacy Not used here — worth noting when a technique is absent too; not every setting uses every tool.

3 · Practice Passage

1When Iqbal had left for school that morning, the garden had been ordinary — washing on the line, a blackbird arguing with next door's cat, sunlight catching the greenhouse glass. He'd barely noticed any of it.

2Now, coming home, the washing line was bare, swinging slightly though there was no wind. The greenhouse door stood open, which it never did, and the glass no longer caught the light — clouds had rolled in low and grey, pressing down over the rooftops as if they, too, were waiting for something to happen. Iqbal stood at the gate a moment longer than he needed to.

1. Identify one example of pathetic fallacy in paragraph 2 and explain the feeling it creates.
2. Compare the garden in paragraph 1 to the garden in paragraph 2. What has changed, and what might this contrast signal to the reader?
3. "The greenhouse door stood open, which it never did." Explain why this detail might be an example of foreshadowing.
4. "Iqbal stood at the gate a moment longer than he needed to." Using the setting details above, infer how Iqbal is feeling in this moment, and support your answer with evidence.
Apply this every time: When a setting appears twice in a passage, always compare the two versions directly — the difference between them is often where the real meaning is, not just the description itself.

Marking Notes (for the tutor)

  1. Q1: "clouds... pressing down over the rooftops as if they, too, were waiting for something to happen" — creates a tense, ominous mood.
  2. Q2: From ordinary/bright/unremarkable to bare/still/grey and disturbed (open greenhouse). Signals that something has happened while Iqbal was at school — a change/disruption to the normal routine of the house.
  3. Q3: The door is explicitly marked as unusual ("which it never did"), a deliberate deviation from the normal that suggests something out of the ordinary has occurred or is about to be revealed.
  4. Q4: Should infer unease/apprehension, supported by the unsettling setting details and his hesitation at the gate — reluctance to go in and find out what's changed.
