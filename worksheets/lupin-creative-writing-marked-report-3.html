Annotated extract

Which vehicle should I pick?

I had been thinking about none of this one hour ago. I was supposed to be driving to France by car (because there were no trains available); however, when I got to start my car I realised I had been robbed of some of the most important components of a car1. So now I had two options: go on a boat or an aeroplane. I was terrified of both of them.

In the end, I chose to be on an aeroplane as I had a feeling I would vomit on a boat. "Hi could I get a taxi to the airport. Really, thanks!"2 I called while waiting on the large, dusty, grey pavement.

Half an hour later: I was at the airport, post security and waiting for my flight. During the wait, my hands shook like I was holding glass, my legs were jelly and fear crept up my spine. Finally after the wait I was on the plane.

My biggest fear.

For every jolt on it3 after takeoff, I was worried that the plane would go plummeting straight towards the ground. I held on to the seat handrest4 until my knuckles were snow white. Whoosh! the plane would go5 every once in a while, making my heart skip a beat.

Eventually I arrived in one piece and was the first to leave the pearl plane6...

Numbered corrections

#TypeIssueFix
1 Reference You've already told us it's your car — calling it "a car" the second time reads like a different, random car. "robbed of some of the most important components of my car"
2 Punctuation A spoken request needs a comma after the greeting and a question mark, not a full stop. "Hi, could I get a taxi to the airport, please? Thanks so much!"
3 Clarity "on it" is vague this far from "plane" — cutting it is cleaner than patching it. "For every jolt after takeoff"
4 Wrong word The part of a seat you grip isn't called a "handrest" — that word doesn't exist in English. "I held on to the armrest"
5 Missing word / capital "The plane would go" is missing what it actually does — and a new sentence after "Whoosh!" needs a capital letter. "Whoosh! The plane would lurch every once in a while"
6 Leftover word "Pearl" doesn't belong here — looks like a word that should have been crossed out fully wasn't. "the first to leave the plane..."

Strengths

A real structural idea, not just a story
  • Splitting the piece with a bold subheading ("My biggest fear.") is a magazine/article device — it signals a shift in focus without needing a clumsy linking sentence.
  • "Half an hour later:" uses a colon to jump time cleanly, echoing the confident one-word-sentence trick from your last piece.
  • Three well-chosen descriptors again — "large, dusty, grey pavement" — you're clearly using this technique on purpose now, not by accident.
Editing as you go
  • Multiple live corrections visible on your own page (fixing verb tense, swapping in "however," catching "unavailable" and rewording it) — this is exactly the habit that will eventually catch things like the "handrest" slip too.
  • A tight triplet of similes for fear — "hands shook like I was holding glass," "legs were jelly," "fear crept up my spine" — shows real control of tone, not just description for its own sake.
  • The turbulence section builds real physical tension (white knuckles, the jolt, the "Whoosh!") — a reader can feel it.

Next steps

This is the same honest note as last time, because it's still the main thing between you and a 20+: (1) read the finished piece back out loud once, slowly — "seat handrest" and "the plane would go" both sound almost right when you're writing at speed, which is exactly why a live read-back catches them and a quick glance doesn't; (2) check any word you've referred to earlier in the piece stays consistent the second time ("my car," not "a car"); and (3) budget the last two minutes for a proper ending sentence instead of trailing off on "..." — you'd already survived the flight here, so one closing line (how it felt to be back on solid ground) would have finished the piece rather than just stopping it.
