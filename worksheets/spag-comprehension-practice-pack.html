1Comprehension — Practice Passage & Full Question Set

A real GL comprehension section mixes 5 sub-skills, not one: retrieval, inference, vocabulary-in-context, grammar-embedded, and literary technique — plus NOT-questions and "voice" questions on harder papers. Every type is included below so nothing is missed in practice.

¶1The shed at the back of her grandfather's garden had not been opened in three years, and when Elsa finally dragged the door across its rusted track, she was met with a smell of oil and old wood. Underneath a tarpaulin, half-buried beneath stacked plant pots, stood a bicycle — its frame furred with rust, one pedal missing entirely, the chain seized solid. It was, her mother had told her more than once, not worth the bother. Elsa disagreed.

¶2She spent the first week simply loosening the chain, dabbing oil onto every stiff link with a rag until her fingers were black and her knuckles raw. Twice she nearly gave up — once when the rear wheel buckled further under a spanner she didn't know how to use, and once when her brother laughed and said she'd have better luck buying a new bike outright. Each time, though, she found herself back in the shed the next morning, sleeves rolled, resolute.

¶3It was while replacing the cracked handlebar grips that she noticed something odd: a thin roll of paper, tucked so deep inside the left grip that it had survived decades untouched. She eased it free with a pair of tweezers. The ink had faded to the colour of weak tea, but the handwriting — small, careful, looping at the tails of its letters — was unmistakably her grandfather's. Whoever rides this next, it read, ride it further than I ever did.

¶4Elsa read the note four times before she folded it back along its original creases and slid it into her pocket, not the grip. When the bicycle was finally roadworthy — three weeks, one replacement pedal, and more oil than she cared to count — she wheeled it out onto the lane behind the house and rode it in a slow, wobbling circle, the note pressing flat against her hip with every turn of the pedals.

¶5Her grandfather had died the spring before she was born; she had only ever known him through photographs and her mother's stories. But freewheeling down the lane that evening, the chain finally silent and smooth beneath her, Elsa felt, for the first time, as though she had actually met him.

Retrieval (stated directly in the text)

Retrieval1. According to paragraph 1, how long had the shed been unopened before Elsa went in?
Retrieval2. According to paragraph 3, what tool did Elsa use to free the note from the handlebar grip?
Retrieval3. According to paragraph 4, how long did it take altogether to make the bicycle roadworthy?

Inference (connecting details the passage doesn't spell out)

Inference4. What does paragraph 2 suggest about Elsa's character, given that she returned to the shed each morning despite two separate setbacks?
Inference5. Why might Elsa have chosen to keep the note in her pocket instead of putting it back inside the grip (paragraph 4)?
Inference6. What does the final paragraph suggest about how riding the bicycle made Elsa feel about the grandfather she never met?

5Grammar Reference — Expanded

Word classes

ClassJobExample
Nounnames a thing/person/place/ideabicycle, determination
Verban action or staterestored, felt
Adjectivedescribes a nounrusted, resolute
Adverbdescribes a verb/adjective/adverbslowly, unmistakably
Prepositionshows relationship (place/time/direction)inside, beneath, before
Conjunctionjoins clauses/wordsalthough, because, but
Pronounreplaces a nounshe, it, whoever
Determinerspecifies a nounthe, her, every

Clause & sentence types

  • Simple sentence — one main clause. "Elsa opened the shed."
  • Compound sentence — two main clauses joined by and/but/or/so. "She oiled the chain, and the wheel finally turned."
  • Complex sentence — a main clause + a subordinate clause. "Although she was tired, she kept going."
  • Relative clause — adds information about a noun, starts with who/which/that/whose. "The note, which had faded, was still legible."
  • Non-finite/participial clause — starts with an -ing or -ed form, no subject of its own. "Tucked inside the grip, the note had survived decades."

Common error categories (with the fix)

  • Comma splice — two main clauses joined only by a comma. Wrong: "It rained, we stayed in." Fixed: "It rained, so we stayed in." / "It rained; we stayed in."
  • Subject-verb agreement — "The list of names were long" → should be was (the subject is "list", singular).
  • Dangling modifier — "Running late, the bus was missed." (who was running? unclear) → "Running late, Elsa missed the bus."
  • its / it's — its = possessive ("its frame"), it's = "it is/it has".
  • who / whom — who is the subject ("who rode the bike"), whom is the object ("to whom it was given").
  • fewer / less — fewer for countable nouns ("fewer mistakes"), less for uncountable ("less time").

6Comprehension Technique — Expanded

Reading strategy

  • Read the whole passage once for gist before looking at any question.
  • Underline names, places, and any word that repeats — likely to matter later.
  • Note the paragraph number in the margin as you read — questions reference it directly.
  • For each question, re-read only the relevant paragraph(s), don't rely on memory.

By question type

  • Retrieval — find the exact matching phrase; distractors are real details from elsewhere in the passage, not invented.
  • Inference — the answer isn't written directly; combine two nearby clues. Ask "what does this action/detail show, not just say?"
  • Vocabulary — always check the word's specific meaning in that sentence, not its most common everyday meaning.
  • NOT-questions — check all four options against the text one at a time; three will be verifiably true, one won't appear at all.
  • Voice questions — think about tone across the whole passage, not just the one quoted line.
  • Literary technique — know the definitions cold (see list below) so you're not guessing under time pressure.

Literary techniques — quick definitions

  • Simile — compares using "like" or "as". Metaphor — states one thing IS another.
  • Personification — gives human qualities to something non-human.
  • Imagery — descriptive language appealing to the senses.
  • Symbolism — an object represents a bigger idea (the bicycle = connection to family).
  • Foreshadowing — an early hint of something that happens later.

7Character & Setting — Three Layers, and Show-Not-Tell

Character — 3 layers

  • Physical — appearance, what we see (black fingers, raw knuckles)
  • Emotional — feelings, inner thought (frustration, resolve)
  • Behavioural — actions, what they actually do (returns to the shed each morning)

Setting — 3 layers

  • Physical — concrete detail (the shed, the tarpaulin, the rusted track)
  • Sensory / imagery — smell of oil and old wood
  • Atmosphere / mood — quiet, nostalgic, almost sacred

Show, Not Tell — worked example

Telling (weak)Showing (strong)
Elsa was determined.Each time she failed, she found herself back in the shed the next morning, sleeves rolled.
The shed was old and neglected.The door dragged across its rusted track; a smell of oil and old wood hung in the air.
