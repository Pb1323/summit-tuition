Name:  Date set:  Due: 

Choose ONE of the following

  1. NarrativeWrite a story that begins with someone finding something that was never meant to be found.
  2. DescriptiveDescribe a workshop or studio belonging to someone who has just died, seen through the eyes of the person clearing it out.
  3. Narrative — dual perspectiveWrite a story told from two different characters' points of view about the same short event, in two clearly separated sections.
Choose the one you can do best, not the one that sounds most impressive. If you pick option 3, both sections need to be genuinely different in what they notice and feel — not just the same scene retold with names swapped.

Technique required

Whichever you choose

A clear structural shape appropriate to your choice: full narrative arc for options 1 and 3; wide→narrow→lasting-image shape for option 2.
At least 3 pieces of figurative language (simile, metaphor, personification) that suit the tone you're building, not randomly inserted.
Vary sentence length and structure — deliberately include at least one very short sentence for impact.
Precise, specific vocabulary throughout — avoid vague words like "nice," "big," "sad," "went" where a sharper choice exists.

If you chose Option 1 (the find)

Establish clearly, without over-explaining, why the object was "never meant to be found" — the reader should feel the weight of it.
End on a consequence or decision, not just the moment of discovery itself.

If you chose Option 2 (the workshop)

No plot, no dialogue — this is pure description filtered through one person's grief, so let their emotion shape which details you choose to linger on.
At least one object described in enough sensory detail that the reader understands what it meant to its owner without being told directly.

If you chose Option 3 (dual perspective)

Clearly separate the two sections (a line break, a heading, or a change in font/format) so the reader knows the perspective has shifted.
Each perspective should notice or misunderstand something different about the same moment — that gap is the whole point of the task.

Your writing

Checklist — go over it again

  1. Does your ending actually end, or does it trail off? Read the last two sentences alone — do they work as a closing image?
  2. Check tense consistency throughout (especially if you switched perspective for option 3).
  3. No clichés — check for overused phrases and replace with your own image.
  4. Every pronoun clearly refers to a named person or object — no ambiguous "it" or "they."
  5. Punctuation for dialogue (if used) is correctly placed inside speech marks with a new line for each new speaker.
  6. No word repeated in the same sentence or the one right after it — swap for a synonym.
