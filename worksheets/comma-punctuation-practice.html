A dedicated deep-dive on commas — the single most error-prone punctuation mark in the SPaG/Punctuation section. Covers every comma rule, then drills each one with real practice sentences (segment-format, matching the real exam) plus an "insert the missing comma" set.

1Comma Rules — Full Reference

RuleExplanationExample
Listsseparates 3+ items in a listShe bought apples, bread, milk and cheese.
Oxford comma (optional but consistent)comma before "and"/"or" in the final list item — pick one style and stay consistentapples, bread, and cheese (vs. apples, bread and cheese — both accepted, but be consistent)
Joining two main clausescomma + coordinating conjunction (and/but/or/so/yet) between two complete sentencesIt started to rain, so we went inside.
Comma splice (ERROR)two main clauses joined by a comma with NO conjunction — always wrong✗ It rained, we went inside. → ✓ It rained, so we went inside. / It rained; we went inside.
After an introductory word/phrasecomma after a fronted adverbial, subordinate clause, or interjection opening a sentenceAlthough it was raining, she went outside. / Eventually, she gave up.
Around non-essential (parenthetical) informationa clause that could be removed without changing the core meaning — commas on BOTH sidesMy brother, who is younger than me, plays football.
NOT around essential (restrictive) informationa clause that IS needed to identify what's being talked about — no commasThe boy who scored the winning goal is my brother. (no commas — identifies which boy)
Direct addressseparates the name of the person being spoken toCan you pass the salt, please, Tom?
Before a quotationintroduces direct speechShe said, "I'm nearly finished."
Separating coordinate adjectivestwo adjectives of equal weight, both describing the same noun equallya cold, wet morning (but: a bright red car — no comma, "bright" modifies "red")
Dates and addressesseparates parts of a date or address15 March, 2026 / London, England

2The Comma Splice — Special Focus

The single most common comma error on real papers. A comma splice happens when two complete sentences (main clauses) are joined by ONLY a comma, with no conjunction.
Wrong (comma splice)Fixed — 4 ways
The bus was late, everyone waited outside.1. Add a coordinating conjunction: ...late, so everyone waited outside.
2. Use a semicolon: ...late; everyone waited outside.
3. Split into two sentences: ...late. Everyone waited outside.
4. Subordinate one clause: Because the bus was late, everyone waited outside.

6Answer Key

Segment format (comma mistakes)

C1: A — comma splice (needs "so"/full stop, not a bare comma)
C2: A — "who lives in Leeds" is non-essential, needs commas both sides: "My sister, who lives in Leeds,"
C3: A — needs a comma after the introductory clause: "Although she was nervous,"
C4: N — no mistake (correctly listed, Oxford comma used consistently)
C5: A — "our new captain" is parenthetical, needs commas both sides: "Marcus, our new captain,"
C6: A — needs a comma before the closing quotation mark: "I'll be there by six,"
C7: A — "bright" and "red" aren't equal coordinate adjectives here ("bright" modifies "red"), no comma needed: "The bright red kite"
C8: A — direct address needs a comma: "Please, Grandad,"

Insert the missing comma

1. Before we left the house, we checked every window twice.
2. My uncle, who taught me to swim, is coming to the party.
3. The match was cancelled, so the team trained indoors instead.
4. Yes, I would love to come to the concert with you.
5. No comma needed — "tall old" are coordinate but read naturally without one; accept either.
6. Well, I suppose we could try the earlier train instead.
7. No comma — "that bit the postman" is essential, it identifies which dog.
8. Once the rain had stopped, the children ran back outside to play.

Comma splice repair (sample fixes)

1. The lights went out, so nobody could see a thing. (conjunction)
2. She trained every single day; she was determined to win. (semicolon)
3. The exam was difficult. Most students still finished on time. (full stop)
4. Because he forgot his umbrella, he got soaked walking home. (subordination)
