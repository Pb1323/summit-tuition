Every written-answer comprehension question
Your Comprehension Operating System sheet (7 August) covers how to eliminate distractor traps on multiple-choice comprehension — that's the GL/CEM-style paper. This sheet is different: it's for written-answer comprehension, where nobody hands you four options and there's no elimination safety net. Latymer's paper is written-response throughout. Dame Alice Owen's and QE Barnet both mix some short written-answer questions into their comprehension sections too. Marks are awarded per distinct point you make, in the words you actually write — so the structure of your answer matters as much as knowing the right idea.

The One Rule That Matters Most

Marks-to-points

Before you write a single word, look at the marks in brackets. A question worth 3 marks needs three genuinely distinct points — not one good point said three different ways. A question worth 1 mark needs exactly one clear point, stated plainly, with no padding.

This is the single biggest source of dropped marks in written-response papers: strong readers who understand the passage perfectly but write a beautiful one-idea paragraph for a 4-mark question, and lose three marks they were fully capable of getting.

Answer Templates, By Question Type

1 · Retrieval / short factual
Usually 1–2 marks · "What did X do when…", "Where was X when…"
State the fact. If the question says "in your own words," you must paraphrase — copying the exact phrase from the text scores zero even if it's the right fact.
[Fact], because/when [supporting detail from the passage, in your own words].
2 · "In your own words" / paraphrase
Usually 2–3 marks · one mark per point you successfully reword
The no-lifting rule: you cannot reuse three or more consecutive words from the original text and still score that point. Swap the key words for genuine synonyms — don't just rearrange the same words into a different order, that's still lifting.
Original: "she hesitated, unwilling to admit the mistake" → Your words: "she paused, reluctant to own up to what she'd got wrong"
3 · Explain / Why
Usually 2–3 marks · Point–Evidence–Explain
Never just quote and stop — a quotation with no explanation attached scores the evidence mark at most, not the explain mark.
Point: [your point] — Evidence: "[short embedded quotation]" — Explain: this shows/suggests [why it proves your point].
4 · Language / effect analysis
Usually 3–4 marks · one mark for technique, one for quotation, one or two for effect
Naming a technique ("the writer uses a metaphor") is worth almost nothing on its own. The marks are in explaining what the specific word choice does to the reader.
The writer's use of "[word/phrase]" suggests [connotation/image], which creates an impression of [effect] and makes the reader feel/picture [reader response].
Watch for: don't drift into retelling the plot — every sentence in a language-effect answer should be doing analytical work, not summary.
5 · Structure / organisation
Usually 3–4 marks · order-of-reveal, then its effect
Track what information the writer gives the reader first, second, third — and why that order was chosen rather than a different one.
The writer withholds/reveals [detail] until [point in the extract]. This has the effect of [suspense/sympathy/surprise/dramatic irony] because the reader [what the reader now knows or feels that they didn't before].
6 · Character / "what do we learn about X"
Usually 4–6 marks · two or three separate Point–Evidence–Explain blocks
Treat this as several mini-answers, not one long paragraph. Each point needs its own piece of evidence — reusing the same quotation for two "different" points only scores once.
Firstly, [character trait] — shown when "[quote]," which suggests [explain]. In addition, [second, genuinely different trait] — shown when "[quote]," which suggests [explain].
7 · Summary
Usually 4–6 marks · one mark per distinct, correct point covered
Coverage beats elegance. List the separate events/ideas in your own words; order roughly follows the passage but doesn't have to be exact. Don't repeat a point using different phrasing — the examiner is counting distinct ideas, not sentences.
[Point 1, own words]. [Point 2, own words — must be a genuinely new idea, not a restatement of point 1]. [Point 3, own words]…

Worked Example

"Mia had checked the running order twice already, but she checked it a third time anyway, because the alternative was sitting still with the thought that kept circling back: the jingle file on the desk wasn't the one she remembered exporting."
Why did Mia check the running order a third time? (2 marks)
Point 1 (1 mark): She had a nagging worry/doubt about the jingle file — "the thought that kept circling back." Point 2 (1 mark): Checking again was a way of avoiding sitting with that worry — "the alternative was sitting still with the thought."
What does "circling back" suggest about Mia's state of mind? (2 marks)
Technique/quote (1 mark): "circling back" suggests something repetitive, going round and round, like a thought she can't switch off. Effect (1 mark): this creates an impression of mounting anxiety rather than a single passing doubt, making the reader sense her unease is building, not settled.
